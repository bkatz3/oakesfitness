# How Strong Should You Be at 60? Realistic Strength Standards

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

The internet loves a single number at 60: bodyweight squat, two-plate deadlift, or a grip target that supposedly proves you are "strong enough." That is the wrong frame. Strength at 60 is not one gym PR. It is a stack of functional floors, population norms, and your own progressive training record. Hit the floors that protect independence, then keep building from where you are.

## What Does "Strong Enough" Actually Mean at 60?

It means three different things, and people keep mixing them up.

1. **Clinical floor.** The lowest strength that flags probable sarcopenia or elevated fall risk. Falling under it is a reason to act, not a lifelong identity.
2. **Population norm.** Where an average 60-year-old lands on grip or sit-to-stand. Useful for orientation. Not a training ceiling.
3. **Training standard.** What you can lift, carry, and stand up with after months of progressive work. This one is personal. Your history, joints, and training age set the starting line.

A systematic review of grip dynamometry found low grip consistently predicted higher mortality, disability, and longer hospital stays in middle-aged and older adults ([Bohannon, 2008, *Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy*, PMID 18489802](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18489802/)). That is why clinics use simple strength screens. It is also why [grip strength predicts longevity after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-06-03-why-grip-strength-predicts-how-long-youll-live-after-50) without meaning a hand gripper is your whole program.

## What Grip Strength Should You Aim For at 60?

Use two layers: a clinical cut-point, then a normal range for your age.

The revised European sarcopenia consensus (EWGSOP2) treats low grip as the entry screen for probable sarcopenia: under about 60 pounds (27 kg) in men and under about 35 pounds (16 kg) in women ([Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019, *Age and Ageing*, PMID 30312372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30312372/)). Those numbers come from life-course British grip norms, where a T-score style cut near −2.5 mapped to those thresholds ([Dodds et al., 2014, *PLoS One*, PMID 25474696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25474696/)).

Dodds and colleagues pooled 60,803 grip observations from 49,964 adults across 12 British studies. At age 60, median grip was about 99 pounds (45 kg) in men and about 60 pounds (27 kg) in women. The 10th-to-90th centile band ran roughly 73–123 pounds (33–56 kg) for men and 40–75 pounds (18–34 kg) for women. Peak strength had already been years earlier (about 112 pounds / 51 kg in men in their 30s; about 68 pounds / 31 kg in women), so a 60-year-old "average" is already a post-peak average.

U.S. NIH Toolbox norms cover ages 18 to 85 and show the same age decline pattern, with mean dominant-hand grip ranging from about 110 pounds (49.7 kg) in young men down to about 41 pounds (18.7 kg) for nondominant grip in women 75 to 79 ([Wang et al., 2018, *Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy*, PMID 29792107](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29792107/)).

| Grip at ~60 | Clinical concern (EWGSOP2) | Rough "middle of the pack" (Dodds median) |
|---|---|---|
| Men | Under ~60 lb (27 kg) | ~99 lb (45 kg) |
| Women | Under ~35 lb (16 kg) | ~60 lb (27 kg) |

Stay above the clinical floor with room to spare. Then chase your own year-over-year trend. A sharp drop matters more than beating a stranger's Instagram deadlift.

## How Fast Should You Stand From a Chair at 60?

Lower-body strength shows up fastest in sit-to-stand, not on a power rack selfie.

EWGSOP2 flags five chair rises taking longer than 15 seconds as low strength ([Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019, *Age and Ageing*, PMID 30312372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30312372/)). In a large French cohort of community adults 65 and older, a five-times sit-to-stand time over 15 seconds predicted recurrent falls ([Buatois et al., 2008, *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*, PMID 18808608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18808608/)).

A descriptive meta-analysis of elders put average five-repetition times near 11.4 seconds at 60 to 69, 12.6 seconds at 70 to 79, and 14.8 seconds at 80 to 89 ([Bohannon, 2006, *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, PMID 17037663](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17037663/)). So at 60, finishing five rises in about 11 seconds or faster is ordinary community performance. Crossing 15 seconds is the red flag, not the training goal.

Arms crossed. Same chair height each time. Stand fully between reps. Time the fifth sit. That screen pairs well with [balance work for fall prevention](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-07-06-how-to-improve-balance-after-50-to-prevent-falls) and with [power training](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-09-why-power-fades-faster-than-strength-after-50), because chair speed is as much about force rate as peak force.

## Do You Need a Bodyweight Squat or Deadlift Number?

No universal "must lift X at 60" standard exists for general adults, and inventing one would be marketing, not evidence.

What does exist is clear programming guidance. The NSCA position statement on resistance training for older adults recommends progressive loading, typically two to three days per week, with healthy older lifters often working toward multi-set work in the roughly 6-to-12-rep range and intensities that can reach about 70 to 85 percent of a 1-rep max when form and joints allow ([Fragala et al., 2019, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, PMID 31343601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/)). Power work at lighter loads is encouraged for function. Training to failure is not required.

Competition powerlifting norms show trained people stay remarkably strong deep into later decades, but those databases describe drug-tested meet lifters, not a homework assignment for every 60-year-old ([van den Hoek et al., 2024, *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport*, PMID 39060209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39060209/)). Use them as proof that progressive training still works after 60, not as your entry ticket. [Building muscle at 70](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-08-is-it-too-late-to-build-muscle-at-70) is still on the table. Your standard is progressive: add load or reps on squat or sit-to-stand variations, hinge, push, pull, and loaded carries over months.

A practical gym scorecard for a healthy 60-year-old beginner or returner:

| Pattern | Realistic early target | How you judge it |
|---|---|---|
| Sit-to-stand / squat | Bodyweight chair stand clean, then a goblet load you can progress | Five rises under ~12 seconds; then add load |
| Hinge | Trap-bar or Romanian hinge you can own for sets of 6–10 | Smooth lockout, no back round under fatigue |
| Push / pull | A press and a row you can progress for months | Matching effort both sides, pain-free |
| Carry / grip | Farmer carry for 30–40 seconds | Hands and trunk stay solid; see [grip movements](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-08-28-10-movements-to-improve-your-grip-strength-after-50) |

Relative strength ratios from competitive lifting are optional later goals. They are not the definition of "strong enough" for independence.

## How Do You Build Toward Those Standards This Week?

Test, then train. Do not guess from a chart alone.

1. Measure grip on a hand dynamometer if you can get one (clinic, PT office, or gym). Record both hands. Convert and track in the same units every time.
2. Time a five-times sit-to-stand on a standard chair. Note whether you needed arm push-off.
3. Pick two to three lifting days. Use the NSCA pattern: major movements, progressive loads, stop short of form breakdown. [Heavier or lighter both work when effort progresses](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-08-11-should-you-lift-heavy-or-light-weights-after-50).
4. Re-test grip and chair stand every four to eight weeks. The trend is the standard that matters.

If you have uncontrolled blood pressure, recent surgery, dizziness, acute joint swelling, or known osteoporosis with clearance questions, get medical input before maximal testing or heavy loading. These screens are training tools, not a diagnosis of [sarcopenia](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-05-04-what-is-sarcopenia) or fall risk by themselves.

## Key Takeaways

- "Strong enough" at 60 is not one deadlift number; it is clearing clinical floors, sitting near or above age norms on simple screens, and progressing your own training.
- EWGSOP2 flags probable low strength at grip under about 60 pounds (27 kg) in men and 35 pounds (16 kg) in women, and at five chair rises slower than 15 seconds.
- In British population norms, median grip at age 60 is about 99 pounds in men and 60 pounds in women, well above those clinical cut-points but below midlife peaks.
- Average five-times sit-to-stand time is about 11.4 seconds at ages 60 to 69; times over 15 seconds are linked to recurrent falls in adults 65 and older.
- Low grip predicts higher mortality, disability, and harder recoveries after hospital stays, which is why it belongs in a yearly check alongside blood pressure-style thinking.
- NSCA guidance for older adults supports progressive resistance training two to three days per week; powerlifting percentile charts describe competitors, not a universal homework standard.
- Retest grip and chair stand every one to two months and treat progressive squat, hinge, push, pull, and carry loads as your personal strength standard.
