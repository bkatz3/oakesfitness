# Can Strength Training Improve Cholesterol Levels After 50?

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Cholesterol talk after 50 usually points to diet, walking, and medication. Strength training gets filed under muscle and bone, as if the blood panel were someone else's job. That split is outdated. Resistance training reliably moves total cholesterol and LDL in older adults — especially when those numbers start high — while HDL and triglycerides are more mixed. It is a real lever. It is not a substitute for the clinician who ordered the labs.

## Does Strength Training Actually Change Cholesterol After 50?

Yes for total cholesterol and LDL. The picture for HDL and triglycerides depends on the population and the program.

A 2023 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials in older adults (988 participants) compared aerobic training, resistance training, combined training, and high-intensity interval work on the full lipid panel ([Yun et al., 2023, *Medicine*, PMID 37478257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478257/)). Resistance training alone significantly lowered total cholesterol (standardized mean difference −0.46) and LDL (SMD −1.75). Combined aerobic-plus-resistance training also lowered total cholesterol and LDL. Aerobic training moved the widest set of markers — including triglycerides down and HDL up — which is why walking and lifting both belong in the week, not in a rivalry.

In postmenopausal women specifically, a separate 2023 meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials found resistance training lowered total cholesterol by about 11.5 mg/dl, LDL by about 8.5 mg/dl, and triglycerides by about 6.6 mg/dl ([He et al., 2023, *European Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Reproductive Biology*, PMID 37421743](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37421743/)). Those are clinic-readable drops, not rounding errors on a printout.

Individual trials land in the same direction. In obese postmenopausal women (mean age about 66), 12 weeks of whole-body lifting three days per week lowered total cholesterol, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol versus controls, without a required change in body weight ([Wooten et al., 2011, *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID 21086242](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21086242/)). In older men, a one-year randomized trial of three weekly resistance sessions lowered LDL by about 0.2 mmol/L (roughly 8 mg/dl) versus an active walking control and added about 0.9 kg of lean mass ([James et al., 2016, *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, PMID 27590313](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27590313/)).

## How Big Are the Changes — and Who Sees Them?

Bigger when lipids or body fat are already elevated, and clearer for LDL and total cholesterol than for HDL.

In the postmenopausal meta-analysis, women who entered with hypercholesterolemia saw about a 12 mg/dl drop in total cholesterol; those with obesity saw about a 19 mg/dl drop ([He et al., 2023, PMID 37421743](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37421743/)). When baseline LDL was at least 130 mg/dl, the LDL reduction was about 14 mg/dl. Shorter interventions (under 16 weeks) showed larger average total-cholesterol and triglyceride shifts than longer ones in that pooled sample — a reminder that early lab changes are common when people actually train, not proof that the effect vanishes if you keep going.

A 12-week progressive program in 236 adults (mean age about 74) at 75 to 80 percent of one-rep max, three days per week, reported mean drops of about 26 mg/dl in total cholesterol, 18 mg/dl in LDL, and 12 mg/dl in triglycerides ([Arnarson et al., 2014, *Aging Clinical and Experimental Research*, PMID 24293371](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24293371/)). That study was a training cohort, not a randomized comparison against a no-lift control, so treat the size as what happened under supervised progressive loading, not as a guaranteed gap versus sitting still. Fat loss and lean-mass gain tracked with better triglyceride changes; people already on lipid-lowering drugs saw larger total-cholesterol and LDL reductions during the same program.

That last point matters for honesty: lifting can sit alongside medication. It is not a reason to change or stop a prescribed statin on your own.

| Study / synthesis | Who | What moved with resistance training |
|---|---|---|
| Yun et al., 2023 (20 RCTs) | Older adults, n=988 | TC and LDL down; TG and HDL not significant for RE alone |
| He et al., 2023 (19 RCTs) | Postmenopausal women | TC ≈ −11.5, LDL ≈ −8.5, TG ≈ −6.6 mg/dl |
| Wooten et al., 2011 | Obese postmenopausal women, 12 weeks | TC, LDL, non-HDL lower vs control |
| James et al., 2016 | Older men, 12 months | LDL ≈ −0.2 mmol/L vs walking control; lean mass up |
| Arnarson et al., 2014 | Adults ~74 years, 12 weeks | TC/LDL/TG down in-program; body-comp shifts predicted TG |

## What About HDL and Triglycerides?

HDL is the weak spot for lifting-only programs. Triglycerides respond more when fat mass falls or when aerobic work is in the mix.

In Yun's older-adult meta-analysis, resistance training alone did not significantly raise HDL or lower triglycerides; aerobic training did both ([Yun et al., 2023, PMID 37478257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478257/)). In He's postmenopausal pool, HDL changes were small overall, and in women with obesity the pooled HDL change was slightly downward — not the "good cholesterol up" story people expect from exercise headlines ([He et al., 2023, PMID 37421743](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37421743/)). James's year-long trial in older men improved LDL without a clear fasting triglyceride or chylomicron win ([James et al., 2016, PMID 27590313](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27590313/)).

So if your panel's main problem is low HDL or high triglycerides, [strength training after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-03-23-why-strength-training-is-non-negotiable-after-50) still earns its place for muscle, blood pressure, and LDL — and you should still keep aerobic work in the week. The same body-composition shifts that show up after 50 ([why body composition changes](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-04-13-body-composition-changes-after-50)) are part of why fat loss and lean-mass gain show up as triglyceride predictors in older lifters.

## Is Lifting Enough on Its Own?

No. It is one lifestyle tool next to diet, aerobic work, sleep, and medical care — not a replacement for any of them.

Yun's comparison is blunt: aerobic training produced the broadest lipid improvements in older adults, while resistance training still moved total cholesterol and LDL ([Yun et al., 2023, PMID 37478257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478257/)). Combined training also lowered total cholesterol and LDL. That is an argument for both, which matches how [strength training lowers blood pressure after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-08-27-how-does-strength-training-lower-blood-pressure-after-50): lifting changes resting cardiometabolic numbers even though people still associate "heart work" with cardio only.

A fasting lipid panel is a medical measurement. Interpreting it, setting targets, and deciding on medication belong with the clinician who knows your history. [Metabolism after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-04-15-why-metabolism-slows-after-50) and training load affect energy balance, but neither licenses self-adjustment of lipid drugs.

## How Should You Train If Lipids Are Part of the Goal?

Match the dose in the trials: progressive, whole-body, two to three days per week, for months — not a month of random machines.

The postmenopausal and older-adult programs that moved LDL used multi-exercise sessions, often three sets in a moderate-to-hard zone (for example 6 to 8 reps near 75 to 80 percent of one-rep max in Arnarson, or 8-RM sets in Wooten), three days per week, for 12 weeks or longer. James ran a full year. You do not need a maximal single. You need loads you can move with a normal breath and clean form, progressed when reps get easy.

Keep aerobic work in the week because that is where HDL and triglycerides have the clearer evidence. Re-check lipids on the same lab schedule your clinician already uses — not the morning after a hard session — so you are measuring the chronic pattern, not acute noise.

## Key Takeaways

- Resistance training after 50 is not only a muscle and bone tool; pooled trials show it lowers total cholesterol and LDL in older adults and postmenopausal women.
- In 19 randomized trials in postmenopausal women, average drops were about 11.5 mg/dl for total cholesterol, 8.5 mg/dl for LDL, and 6.6 mg/dl for triglycerides, with larger LDL shifts when baseline LDL was already high.
- In 20 randomized trials in older adults, resistance training alone lowered total cholesterol and LDL; aerobic training produced broader improvements including triglycerides and HDL.
- HDL responses to lifting-only programs are inconsistent; do not expect resistance training alone to fix a low-HDL pattern.
- Individual trials in older men and postmenopausal women support LDL improvement over months to a year when training is progressive and supervised.
- Lifting can sit alongside lipid-lowering medication; changing or stopping prescribed drugs is a clinician decision, not a training decision.
- Practical dose: two to three whole-body sessions per week for months, plus aerobic work, with lipids rechecked on your usual medical schedule.
