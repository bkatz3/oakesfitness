# What Is a DEXA Scan and Should You Get One After 50?

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

A DEXA scan gets treated as either a vanity body-fat number or something only your mother needed after she broke a wrist. It is neither. It is a low-dose X-ray measurement of bone density, and in some settings body composition, that tells you how much margin you have before a fall turns into a fracture. After 50, that margin shrinks quietly, which is exactly why a measurement beats a guess.

## What Does a DEXA Scan Actually Measure?

DEXA (also written DXA) stands for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Two X-ray beams at different energies pass through your body, and because bone, lean tissue, and fat absorb them differently, the machine can separate the three.

There are two common versions, and they answer different questions:

- **Bone density DEXA.** Usually the lumbar spine and hip. This is the clinical test for osteoporosis, ordered by a physician and reported as a T-score.
- **Whole-body composition DEXA.** Reports lean mass, fat mass, and where each sits. Often offered by private clinics and performance labs, and used in research to estimate appendicular (arms and legs) muscle mass.

The European consensus on sarcopenia lists DXA as one of the accepted ways to measure muscle quantity when confirming a sarcopenia diagnosis, alongside strength testing as the first screen ([Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019, *Age and Ageing*, PMID 30312372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30312372/)). That is a useful detail: the same group put low strength first, ahead of low muscle mass. A scan confirms; it does not replace [knowing what sarcopenia is and how it shows up](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-05-04-what-is-sarcopenia) in daily function.

## How Do You Read a T-Score?

The T-score compares your bone density to a healthy young adult of the same sex. Each whole number is one standard deviation.

| T-Score | Category | What It Means for Training |
|---|---|---|
| -1.0 or higher | Normal | Train progressively; keep loading bone on purpose |
| Between -1.0 and -2.5 | Low bone mass (osteopenia) | Prioritize heavy, well-coached strength and impact work |
| -2.5 or lower | Osteoporosis | Strength training still matters; technique, supervision, and exercise selection matter more |
| Any score plus a fragility fracture | Higher risk regardless of number | Talk to your clinician before changing the program |

The table is a training lens, not a treatment plan. Whether a number calls for medication is a conversation for your physician, who will usually combine it with age, history, and other risk factors.

## Does a Normal-Looking Number Mean You're Safe?

Not necessarily, and this is the part most people miss.

In the National Osteoporosis Risk Assessment, 149,524 postmenopausal women aged 50 to 104 had bone density measured with peripheral devices (heel, finger, or forearm) and reported new fractures over the following year ([Siris et al., 2004, *Archives of Internal Medicine*, PMID 15159268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15159268/)). Women in the osteoporosis range had the highest fracture rates, but 82 percent of the women who fractured had T-scores better than -2.5. Most fractures happened in the much larger group with low but not osteoporotic bone mass.

Two cautions keep that honest: the scans were peripheral rather than spine and hip, and fractures were self-reported. The takeaway still holds. A T-score in the osteopenia range is not a pass. It is a reason to train with more intent, not less.

## Who Should Get a Bone Density Scan, and When?

The most recent US Preventive Services Task Force recommendation, based on a systematic review of adults 40 and older without known osteoporosis or prior fragility fracture, recommends screening for ([Nicholson et al., 2025, *JAMA*, PMID 39808425](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39808425/)):

- All women 65 and older
- Postmenopausal women younger than 65 who are at increased risk based on a clinical risk assessment

For men, the Task Force concluded the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against routine screening. That is not the same as "men don't need it." It means the decision depends more on individual risk, so a man in his 60s with a prior fracture, long-term steroid use, or a family history worth mentioning should raise it with his doctor rather than assume it doesn't apply.

If you are a postmenopausal woman under 65 and nobody has assessed your fracture risk yet, that conversation is the first step, not the scan itself.

## Is the Radiation Something to Worry About?

For a standard bone density scan in an adult, no. A review of X-ray methods used in osteoporosis described DXA radiation doses as very low, with CT-based bone techniques delivering considerably more ([Damilakis et al., 2010, *European Radiology*, PMID 20559834](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20559834/)). The same authors make the reasonable point that any X-ray should still be clinically justified, which is a good reason to get scans on a schedule rather than out of curiosity every few months.

## What Should You Do With the Results?

A scan is only worth getting if it changes what you do. For most people over 50, the action item is the same regardless of the exact number: load your bones progressively and consistently.

The clearest trial on this point is LIFTMOR. Researchers randomized 101 postmenopausal women (average age 65) with low to very low bone mass to either 8 months of twice-weekly, 30-minute supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training (5 sets of 5 at over 85 percent of one-rep max) or a home-based low-intensity program ([Watson et al., 2018, *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research*, PMID 28975661](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28975661/)). The high-intensity group gained 2.9 percent in lumbar spine bone density while the low-intensity group lost 1.2 percent, improved femoral neck density relative to controls, and outperformed on every functional measure. Only one minor adverse event was reported. The key words are supervised and screened; this was not people loading a barbell alone off a YouTube video.

If your scan shows low bone mass, the posts on [how strength training builds bone density](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-03-05-how-strength-training-builds-bone-density-after-50) and [which exercises are safe with osteoporosis](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-11-exercises-safe-with-osteoporosis) go deeper on how to set that up.

## How to Use a DEXA Scan This Year

- **Ask, don't assume.** If you are a woman 65 or older, or postmenopausal with risk factors, ask your physician whether a bone density scan is due. Men with a prior fracture or other risk factors should ask too.
- **Bring the report, not only the number.** Note which sites were measured (spine, hip, forearm) and the T-score at each. They can differ.
- **Repeat on the same machine when you can.** Small changes are hard to interpret across different scanners, so tracking at the same facility makes a follow-up scan more meaningful.
- **Pair it with strength numbers.** Grip strength, a five-times sit-to-stand, and your working weights on a squat or deadlift pattern tell you how the muscle around that bone is doing.
- **Train two to three days a week with progressive load.** That is the lever the research supports, and it applies whether the scan comes back normal or not.

## Key Takeaways

- A DEXA scan uses two low-dose X-ray beams to separate bone, lean tissue, and fat, and the clinical version measures bone density at the spine and hip to produce a T-score.
- The European sarcopenia consensus accepts DXA as a way to confirm low muscle quantity, but it lists low strength first, so a scan supports rather than replaces strength testing.
- In a cohort of nearly 150,000 postmenopausal women, 82 percent of those who fractured within a year had T-scores better than the osteoporosis cutoff, so an osteopenia result is a reason to train with intent.
- The 2025 USPSTF recommendation calls for bone density screening in all women 65 and older and in younger postmenopausal women at increased risk, and found insufficient evidence to recommend routine screening for men.
- DXA radiation doses are very low compared with CT-based bone imaging, but scans should still be done for a clinical reason on a sensible schedule.
- In the LIFTMOR trial, eight months of supervised heavy resistance and impact training raised lumbar spine bone density by 2.9 percent in postmenopausal women with low bone mass while a low-intensity group lost 1.2 percent.
