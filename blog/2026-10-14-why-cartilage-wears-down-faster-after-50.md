# Why Cartilage Wears Down Faster After 50 (and What Slows It)

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Most people picture cartilage like the tread on a tire: every step, squat, and stair uses up a little more, so the smart move after 50 is to save what you have left. That picture is wrong. Cartilage is living tissue that responds to load, and the bigger threats to it after 50 are a cell that repairs more slowly, weak muscles around the joint, and extra body weight, not movement itself.

## What Is Cartilage, and Why Doesn't It Heal Like Muscle?

Articular cartilage is the smooth, slippery layer that caps the ends of bones inside a joint like the knee or hip. It is mostly water held in place by a mesh of collagen and large sugar-protein molecules called proteoglycans (their sugar chains are glycosaminoglycans, or GAGs). That structure is what lets cartilage absorb load and spring back.

The cells that maintain it, chondrocytes, are sparse, and cartilage has no blood supply of its own. Nutrients reach those cells largely through the fluid that gets pressed in and out of the tissue as the joint is loaded and unloaded. That detail matters for everything that follows: cartilage depends on movement to stay fed.

## Why Does Cartilage Wear Down Faster After 50?

Not because it has been used too much. A review of the aging research from Wake Forest University laid out what actually changes ([Loeser, 2009, *Osteoarthritis and Cartilage*, PMID 19303469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19303469/)):

- **The repair crew slows down.** Older chondrocytes divide less and build less new matrix, but they keep producing inflammatory signals and enzymes that break matrix down. The review describes this as a senescent, or worn-out, cell profile.
- **Oxidative stress drives much of it.** The evidence pointed to stress-induced aging of the cells rather than a fixed internal clock running out.
- **The matrix itself gets stiffer.** Proteins in cartilage accumulate sugar-related crosslinks with age (a process called glycation), which makes the tissue more brittle.

The conclusion of that review is the useful part: older cartilage is less able to keep its balance when it is stressed. Aging doesn't guarantee osteoarthritis. It narrows the margin, so the things that add stress or take away support matter more after 50 than they did at 30.

## Does Exercise Wear Cartilage Out or Protect It?

For the loads most adults over 50 are actually doing, the evidence leans toward protect.

In a four-month randomized trial in Sweden, 45 adults (average age 46) who had part of a knee meniscus removed three to five years earlier, a group at high risk for knee osteoarthritis, were assigned to supervised exercise three times a week or to no intervention ([Roos and Dahlberg, 2005, *Arthritis and Rheumatism*, PMID 16258919](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16258919/)). MRI estimates of cartilage GAG content improved in the exercise group and declined in the controls, and people who increased their activity more tended to improve more. Only 30 participants had both scans, and the group was younger than our readers, so treat it as a strong signal rather than a final word. Still, it is direct evidence that adult human cartilage can adapt to loading.

A later systematic review of nine randomized trials looked at MRI-measured cartilage in people at risk of or already living with knee osteoarthritis ([Bricca et al., 2019, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID 29934429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29934429/)). Most comparisons found no change in cartilage thickness or volume, a few found positive effects, and one found a negative effect. The authors concluded that knee-loading exercise does not appear to harm cartilage, while noting the quality of evidence was low. Read honestly: exercise is not a cartilage-regrowth program, but the fear that it grinds the joint down isn't supported.

## What About Running?

Running is where the tire analogy gets the most airtime. A meta-analysis of 17 studies with more than 114,000 people compared hip and knee osteoarthritis in runners and non-runners ([Alentorn-Geli et al., 2017, *Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy*, PMID 28504066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28504066/)). Osteoarthritis showed up in 3.5 percent of recreational runners, 10.2 percent of sedentary controls, and 13.3 percent of competitive or elite runners.

These were observational studies, so they can't prove cause, and prior injury may explain part of the picture. The shape is still worth noticing: the problem showed up at both ends, too little movement and very high volume, not in the middle.

## What Actually Slows Cartilage Loss After 50?

Three levers have the best support.

### Stronger thigh muscles

The quadriceps act as the knee's shock absorber. An updated meta-analysis of 11 long-term studies with 46,819 adults who did not have knee osteoarthritis at the start found that weak knee extensors raised the odds of developing it ([Øiestad et al., 2022, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID 34916210](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34916210/)). For symptomatic knee osteoarthritis, the odds were about 85 percent higher in weaker women and 43 percent higher in weaker men. The authors rated the evidence as low quality because these are observational studies, but the direction was consistent across sexes and outcomes.

### Less load per step, if you carry extra weight

In 142 older adults with knee osteoarthritis and overweight or obesity, each pound of weight lost was associated with about four pounds less force through the knee per step during walking ([Messier et al., 2005, *Arthritis and Rheumatism*, PMID 15986358](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15986358/)). Multiply that across several thousand steps a day and it adds up.

The follow-up IDEA trial randomized 454 adults aged 55 and older with knee osteoarthritis and a BMI of 27 to 41 to diet, exercise, or both for 18 months ([Messier et al., 2013, *JAMA*, PMID 24065013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24065013/)). The diet-plus-exercise group lost about 11 percent of body weight and had less pain and better function than either diet or exercise alone. The diet groups also had lower knee compressive force and lower levels of IL-6, an inflammation marker, than the exercise-only group. Exercise was part of the winning combination, not the whole answer.

### Regular, moderate loading

Cartilage gets its nutrients through compression and release. Long stretches of sitting take that away. Frequent, moderate loading, the kind you get from walking, cycling, and well-coached strength work, keeps the fluid moving.

## Is Your Training Helping or Hurting Your Cartilage?

| Signal | Likely Helping | Worth Adjusting |
|---|---|---|
| Joint feel the next morning | Same or better than before | Swollen, hot, or noticeably worse |
| Pain during a set | Mild, settles as you warm up | Sharp, or climbs set to set |
| Weekly pattern | Regular moderate sessions | Long layoffs, then big weekend efforts |
| Quad strength trend | Slowly going up | Flat or dropping |
| Body weight trend | Stable or heading where you want it | Creeping up year over year |

Swelling, locking, a joint that gives way, or pain that wakes you at night is a reason to see a clinician before you keep training through it.

## How to Protect Your Cartilage This Week

- **Train your legs two to three days a week.** Squat-pattern work to a comfortable depth, step-ups, split squats, and leg presses. Start with 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 and add load or reps gradually. If squats bother your knees, our guide on [how to squat without knee pain after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-06-02-how-to-squat-without-knee-pain-after-50) covers the setup.
- **Break up long sitting.** Get up and walk for a few minutes every 30 to 60 minutes. Cartilage needs the in-and-out of load to stay fed.
- **Use low-impact cardio on hard days.** Cycling, swimming, or brisk walking keep joints moving without stacking impact on top of a heavy leg day.
- **Keep running if you like it and your joints tolerate it.** Build volume slowly and treat new swelling as a signal to back off, not push through.
- **Work on body weight alongside training if it applies.** In the IDEA trial, diet and exercise together beat either one alone.
- **Warm up the joint before loading it.** Five to ten minutes of easy movement through the range you are about to train.

If you already have a diagnosis, our post on [whether you should exercise with arthritis](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-17-should-you-exercise-with-arthritis) goes further into training with it.

## Key Takeaways

- Cartilage is living tissue that gets its nutrients through the squeeze and release of joint loading, so movement feeds it rather than simply using it up.
- After 50, cartilage cells repair more slowly while still producing breakdown signals, and the matrix stiffens, which leaves the tissue less able to handle stress.
- In a four-month randomized trial in adults at high risk for knee osteoarthritis, supervised exercise three times a week improved an MRI marker of cartilage quality while the control group declined.
- A systematic review of nine randomized trials found knee-loading exercise does not appear to harm cartilage in people at risk of or with knee osteoarthritis, though the evidence quality was low.
- Recreational runners had lower rates of hip and knee osteoarthritis than sedentary adults in a meta-analysis of more than 114,000 people, while competitive runners had the highest rates.
- Weak quadriceps were linked to higher odds of developing knee osteoarthritis in a meta-analysis of 46,819 adults, which makes leg strength one of the clearest protective levers.
- Among older adults with knee osteoarthritis and overweight, each pound lost was associated with about four pounds less knee force per step, and diet plus exercise beat either alone in the IDEA trial.
