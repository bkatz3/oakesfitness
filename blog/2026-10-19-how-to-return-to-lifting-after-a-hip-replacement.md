# How to Return to Lifting After a Hip Replacement

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Plenty of people leave hip replacement surgery believing the new joint is something to protect for the rest of their lives: walk, do the physical therapy sheet, and stay away from the weight room. That gets it backwards. The joint is new, but the muscles around it are not, and the research shows they can stay smaller and weaker for years unless you load them on purpose. After 50, when you are already fighting age-related muscle loss, closing that gap is the whole game.

## Why Are You Still Weak After the Hip Is Fixed?

Because surgery replaces the joint, not the muscle. Most people spend years guarding a painful hip before the operation, and the muscles around it shrink in that time. Removing the pain does not rebuild them.

A Swedish study measured the muscles of 20 patients with osteoarthritis in one hip using CT scans before surgery, at six months, and at two years ([Rasch et al., 2009, *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery, British Volume*, PMID 19407289](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19407289/)). Two years after surgery, the operated side still had a 7 percent smaller iliopsoas (the main hip flexor) and 8.4 percent smaller adductors than the healthy side. The glutes also showed lower density on the scans, a common marker of poorer muscle quality. The authors suggested more intensive rehabilitation might reverse this.

A study from the University of Colorado tested 26 patients at one, three, six, and twelve months after surgery and compared them with 19 adults with healthy hips ([Judd et al., 2014, *Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research*, PMID 23817756](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23817756/)). One month after surgery, hip abductor strength was down about 26 percent and hip flexor and extensor strength about 15 percent. At one year, patients still had 17 percent less knee extensor and 23 percent less knee flexor strength than healthy peers, even though their stair, sit-to-stand, and walking tests were no longer significantly different in this small sample.

Feeling fine and being strong are not the same thing. That leftover deficit stacks on top of the normal decline we described in [what sarcopenia is and why it starts earlier than you think](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-05-04-what-is-sarcopenia).

## Is Heavy Lifting Safe After a Hip Replacement?

In supervised settings, it has been tested and was well tolerated.

In a small Norwegian trial, 24 patients were randomized to conventional rehab alone or conventional rehab plus maximal strength training: leg press and hip abduction with the operated leg, five times a week for four weeks, starting about one week after surgery ([Husby et al., 2009, *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, PMID 19801053](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19801053/)). The strength group gained more on one-rep max leg press and abduction and produced force faster. Walking patterns did not differ.

That trial included only patients under 65. The same group then tested the approach in 60 patients in regular clinical practice ([Winther et al., 2018, *Acta Orthopaedica*, PMID 29493347](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29493347/)). One group trained leg press and abduction at 85 to 90 percent of their max, four sets of five reps, three times a week at a physical therapy clinic for up to three months. The comparison group did usual physical therapy, mostly with light or no load. At three months, the heavy group was 43 kg stronger on the leg press and 3 kg stronger in abduction. At six months the leg press gap was 30 kg. By one year, after the supervised program had ended, the groups no longer differed. Pain, walking distance, and hip function scores were similar throughout.

The honest read: heavier, well-coached work builds strength faster after a hip replacement, and the advantage did not last once the supervised program ended. That is an argument for lifting long after formal rehab ends.

## Does Supervision Matter Early On?

Less than you might think at first. A Danish trial of 73 patients with lower function before surgery compared seven weekly sessions of home exercise with five home sessions plus two supervised progressive resistance sessions for 10 weeks ([Mikkelsen et al., 2014, *Osteoarthritis and Cartilage*, PMID 25305374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25305374/)). Leg extension power improved about the same in both groups. The supervised group did improve more in maximal walking speed and stair climbing.

Early on, doing your exercises consistently matters most. Supervision earns its keep later, when the job shifts to progressing load safely.

## What About Hip Precautions?

Hip precautions are movement limits some surgeons give after surgery, often around deep hip bending, crossing the legs, or turning the leg inward. A systematic review of four randomized trials and five non-randomized studies, 8,835 patients who had a posterior-approach surgery, found there may be little or no difference in early dislocations with or without precautions ([Korfitsen et al., 2023, *Acta Orthopaedica*, PMID 37039064](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37039064/)). The authors concluded the evidence does not support routinely prescribing them, while rating the certainty of that evidence as low to very low.

That explains why instructions vary from surgeon to surgeon. It is not a reason to ignore yours. Follow any precautions you were given for as long as your surgeon says, and ask at your follow-up which positions are cleared before you load deep squats or hinges in the gym.

## When Can You Get Back to Sports and the Gym?

Most people do. A meta-analysis of 57 studies from the Cleveland Clinic found a pooled return-to-sport rate of 85 percent after hip replacement, at an average of about 16 weeks ([Pasqualini et al., 2023, *JBJS Reviews*, PMID 37549241](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37549241/)). The rate was 97 percent for low-intensity sports and 56 percent for high-intensity sports. These are pooled figures from studies of mixed quality, so they describe what people did, not what is safe for you. Ask your surgeon directly about running, jumping, or contact sports.

## How Do You Rebuild Strength After a Hip Replacement?

The timeline below is a typical shape, not a prescription. Your surgeon and physical therapist set the actual pace.

- **Weeks 0 to 6: follow the plan.** Do your PT exercises as prescribed and walk often. Ask your care team before adding seated upper-body work.
- **Weeks 6 to 12, once cleared: start loading.** Two or three strength sessions a week. Sit-to-stand from a box at or above knee height, leg press within your cleared range, glute bridges, banded or side-lying hip abduction, and step-ups on a 4 to 6 inch step. Do 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps and stop each set with 2 or 3 good reps left.
- **Progress with a simple rule.** When you complete every rep with clean form for two sessions in a row, add a small amount of load.
- **Months 3 to 6: get heavier.** Move leg press and machine or cable hip abduction toward sets of 5 or 6 at a heavy but controlled effort. That is close to the four sets of five the larger Norwegian trial used under supervision. Add supported split squats, goblet squats to a box, and farmer carries.
- **Train balance alongside strength.** Single-leg stands near a counter rebuild control on the operated side. Our guide on [how to improve balance after 50 to prevent falls](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-07-06-how-to-improve-balance-after-50-to-prevent-falls) has a progression.
- **Months 6 to 12 and beyond: keep two leg days a week.** Trap bar deadlifts from blocks, step-downs, and squats to your cleared depth. If squats bother your knees, see [how to squat without knee pain after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-06-02-how-to-squat-without-knee-pain-after-50).

## How Do You Know If You Are Pushing Too Hard?

| Signal | Usually fine | Back off and check with your surgeon |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle soreness | Mild, in the thigh or glutes, gone in a day or two | Building session to session |
| Pain during a set | Low, steady, settles as you warm up | Sharp, deep in the groin or hip, or climbing set to set |
| Swelling | Minimal and stable | New or increasing around the hip or thigh |
| Joint feel | Stable under load | Clunking with pain, or a sense the hip might give way |
| Incision area | Healing as expected | Redness, warmth, drainage, or fever |

The right column is a reason to stop training and call your surgeon, not something to train through.

## Key Takeaways

- Hip replacement fixes the joint, not the muscle, and CT scans showed smaller, lower-quality hip muscles on the operated side two years after surgery.
- One year after surgery, patients in a Colorado study still had 17 to 23 percent less thigh strength than healthy peers, even though their function tests were no longer significantly different.
- In two Norwegian randomized trials, heavy leg press and hip abduction training (85 to 90 percent of max in the larger one) was well tolerated and built more strength than usual physical therapy.
- The strength advantage from heavy training had faded by one year after the supervised program ended, which argues for continuing to lift long after formal rehab.
- In the first 10 weeks, consistent home exercise produced similar leg power gains to adding supervised sessions, so early on, doing the work matters more than where.
- A review of 8,835 patients found routine hip precautions may make little difference to early dislocation risk, but evidence certainty was low, so follow your own surgeon's instructions.
- About 85 percent of people return to sport after hip replacement, at an average of about 16 weeks, with far higher rates for low-intensity than high-intensity activities.
