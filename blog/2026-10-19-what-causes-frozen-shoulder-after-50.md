# What Causes Frozen Shoulder After 50 and How Training Helps

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Frozen shoulder is usually explained one of two ways: it goes away on its own in a year or two, so wait it out, or it needs to be stretched hard until it lets go. Neither holds up well. The research shows many shoulders do not fully recover by waiting, and aggressive stretching tends to do worse than steady, gentle work inside your pain limits. Because frozen shoulder shows up most often in your 50s, it is worth knowing what is actually going on in the joint and how training fits around it.

## What Is Frozen Shoulder, Exactly?

Frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis, is a thickening and tightening of the capsule that surrounds the shoulder joint. It is not a muscle strain and it is not the same thing as general shoulder stiffness from sitting at a desk.

A 2022 review describes it as a fibrotic process: cells in the joint capsule shift into a contractile type and lay down extra collagen, with inflammation and new blood vessels and nerve endings. The capsule shrinks and stiffens, so both pain and range of motion suffer. The same review notes that it often appears alongside other systemic conditions or after a period of immobilization, and that diagnosis relies heavily on a physical exam ([Millar et al., 2022, *Nature Reviews Disease Primers*, PMID 36075904](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36075904/)).

The hallmark sign is that the shoulder will not move far even when someone else moves it for you, especially when rotating the arm outward.

## Why Does It Show Up After 50?

The exact trigger is still not well understood, but the pattern of who gets it is consistent.

In a long-term follow-up of 223 people (269 shoulders) with primary frozen shoulder, the average age when symptoms started was 53.4 years, and women were affected about 1.6 times as often as men. Twenty percent eventually had symptoms in both shoulders, though none had the same shoulder come back ([Hand et al., 2008, *Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery*, PMID 17993282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17993282/)).

Metabolic health is part of the picture. A meta-analysis of 18 studies found people with diabetes were about five times more likely than controls to have frozen shoulder, and about 30 percent of people with frozen shoulder had diabetes ([Zreik et al., 2016, *Muscles, Ligaments and Tendons Journal*, PMID 27331029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27331029/)). That is an association, not proof of cause.

Immobilization matters too. A shoulder kept still after a fall or surgery has more time to tighten, and that is the part training can influence most. For the broader picture of why joints lose motion with age, see our post on [why joints get stiff after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-14-why-joints-get-stiff-after-50).

## Does Frozen Shoulder Go Away on Its Own?

Partly, for many people. Fully, for fewer than the old story suggests.

The classic teaching is that frozen shoulder moves through a painful phase, a stiff phase, and a thawing phase, ending in full recovery without treatment. A systematic review that tested that idea found no evidence for progression through those phases to full resolution. Low-quality evidence suggested that untreated shoulders improved somewhat over one to four years, but not completely, and moderate-quality evidence from three randomized trials showed most of the improvement happened early rather than late ([Wong et al., 2017, *Physiotherapy*, PMID 27641499](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27641499/)).

The long-term follow-up above fits that. At an average of 4.4 years after symptoms began, 59 percent had normal or near normal shoulders and 41 percent still had some symptoms. Most of those leftover symptoms were mild, mainly pain, and only 6 percent had severe pain and loss of function. People with the worst symptoms at the start tended to have the worst long-term outcome ([Hand et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17993282/)).

So waiting is not a plan. It is a bet.

## Should You Stretch a Frozen Shoulder Hard?

Usually not, and this is where most well-meaning people go wrong.

In a prospective study of 77 people with frozen shoulder followed for two years, one group got intensive physical therapy with passive stretching and manual mobilization. The other got what the authors called supervised neglect: supportive care and exercises kept within pain limits. At 24 months, 89 percent of the gentle group had normal or near normal painless function, and 64 percent got there within the first year. In the intensive stretching group, only 63 percent reached that mark by 24 months ([Diercks and Stevens, 2004, *Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery*, PMID 15383804](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15383804/)). This was not a randomized trial, so treat the size of the gap with some caution, but the direction is clear.

Movement helps. Cranking on an irritated capsule does not.

## What Does the Treatment Research Say?

No single treatment is a clear winner over the long run, and the more invasive options have not beaten structured, conservative care.

The UK FROST trial randomized 503 adults with frozen shoulder at 35 hospitals to manipulation under anesthesia, arthroscopic capsular release surgery, or early structured physiotherapy with a steroid injection and a graduated home exercise program. At 12 months, none of the three was clinically superior on shoulder pain and function. Capsular release carried more serious adverse events ([Rangan et al., 2020, *The Lancet*, PMID 33010843](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33010843/)).

A meta-analysis of 65 randomized studies with 4,097 participants found that only an injected corticosteroid into the joint showed clear short-term benefit for pain and function. Adding a home exercise program of simple exercises and stretches to that injection was associated with added benefit in the mid-term, and the authors recommended pairing the two for shoulders with symptoms under a year old ([Challoumas et al., 2020, *JAMA Network Open*, PMID 33326025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33326025/)).

Injections and procedures are decisions for you and your physician. Training covers what every approach still relies on: regular, well-dosed movement and a strong body around the shoulder.

## How Training Changes by Stage

| What you notice | What it usually means | Training focus |
|---|---|---|
| Pain at night and at rest, motion starting to shrink | Early, irritable phase | Gentle range within pain limits, keep everything else training, see a clinician |
| Less pain, but the arm will not go overhead or rotate out | Stiff phase | Daily low-load range work, light strength in the range you have |
| Range slowly returning | Recovery | Load through the new range, rebuild overhead and rotation strength |
| Sudden severe pain, weakness, or swelling after an injury | Possibly a different problem | Stop and get it examined |

## How to Train With a Frozen Shoulder This Week

This assumes a clinician has confirmed the diagnosis. If you have not had it examined, start there.

- **Use a pain rule.** Range and strength work should feel like mild stretch or effort, roughly 3 out of 10 or less, and settle back to baseline within an hour or so. If the shoulder is angrier the next morning, you did too much.
- **Move it a little, often.** Two or three short sessions a day of 5 to 10 minutes beat one long, forceful session. Good options are table slides, cane-assisted forward raises, pendulum swings, and gentle outward rotation with a dowel, 10 to 15 easy reps each.
- **Strengthen what you can reach.** Light band rows, band pull-aparts in a comfortable range, and isometric holds for the rotator cuff (pressing gently into a wall for 20 to 30 seconds) keep the muscles working without forcing the capsule.
- **Train the rest of your body normally.** Squats, deadlift variations, lunges, single-arm work on the good side, carries on the good side, and cardio can all continue. Losing months of leg strength to one stiff shoulder is an avoidable cost after 50.
- **Avoid forced end range.** Skip overhead pressing, behind-the-neck positions, heavy hanging, and partner stretching into pain until range returns.
- **Progress by range, not by weight.** As motion comes back, add load gradually in the new range. Overhead work comes last.
- **Track one number.** Once a week, note how high you can reach on a wall with the affected arm. Slow upward movement over weeks is the signal you want.

## Key Takeaways

- Frozen shoulder is a fibrotic thickening and tightening of the shoulder joint capsule, which limits motion even when someone else moves the arm for you.
- In a long-term study of 223 people, frozen shoulder started at an average age of 53.4 and affected women about 1.6 times as often as men.
- A meta-analysis of 18 studies found people with diabetes were about five times more likely to have frozen shoulder, so unexplained shoulder freezing is worth raising with a physician.
- The idea that frozen shoulder reliably resolves on its own is not supported; at an average of 4.4 years, 41 percent of patients still had some symptoms, though most were mild.
- In a two-year study of 77 people, gentle exercise within pain limits led to normal or near normal function in 89 percent, compared with 63 percent after intensive stretching.
- The UK FROST trial of 503 adults found that surgery and manipulation under anesthesia were not clinically superior to structured physiotherapy with a steroid injection at 12 months.
- The practical training approach is frequent, low-pain range work, light strength in the available range, and normal training for the rest of the body.
