# Why Posture Gets Worse After 50 and How Strength Training Fixes It

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Most people treat a rounding upper back as something you either accept with age or fix by reminding yourself to stand up straight. Neither is quite right. Posture after 50 is partly a strength problem, and the muscles that hold your spine upright can be trained like any other muscle. The research says the change is modest but real, and it is easier to slow the curve than to reverse it, which is why your 50s are a good time to start.

## What Actually Happens to Posture After 50?

The main change is in the upper back. The thoracic spine has a natural forward curve, called kyphosis. With age, that curve tends to grow, the head drifts forward, and the shoulders round. When the curve gets past about 40 degrees, researchers call it hyperkyphosis.

It is common. A review in *Annals of Internal Medicine* put the prevalence of hyperkyphosis among older adults at 20% to 40% ([Kado et al., 2007, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, PMID 17785488](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17785488/)). The same review made a point that surprises people: many older adults with the most rounding do not have vertebral fractures. A fracture can cause it, but it is not the only cause.

The timing matters too. In a cross-sectional study of 250 women aged 30 to 79, the biggest jump in the curve showed up between the 50s and the 60s ([Ball et al., 2009, *Osteoporosis International*, PMID 18661090](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18661090/)). That is a snapshot of different women at different ages, not the same women followed over time, but it lines up with what we see in the studio: the 50s are when the change starts to show.

## Why Does It Happen?

A review of age-related hyperkyphosis lists several contributors: low bone mass, vertebral compression fractures, degenerative disc disease, and weakness in the muscles along the back of the spine ([Katzman et al., 2010, *Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy*, PMID 20511692](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20511692/)).

Of those, muscle is the one you have the most control over. In a cross-sectional study of 189 women aged 50 to 80, back extensor strength was linked to the size of the thoracic curve, while bone density was not ([Mika et al., 2005, *Spine*, PMID 15644764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15644764/)). The weakest group had a noticeably larger curve than the women with medium or high back strength. It is a single snapshot, so it cannot prove weakness causes rounding, but it points at the back extensors as a lever worth pulling.

Long hours of sitting and looking down at screens do not help. We are not aware of strong trial data that puts a number on that, so treat it as a practical observation: if you spend most of the day in a forward-rounded position and never train the opposite direction, the rounded position is the one your body gets good at.

## Does Posture Really Matter, or Is It Cosmetic?

It goes beyond looks, though the studies here are observational, so they show links rather than proof.

- In the Rancho Bernardo Study, 1,353 older adults were followed for about four years. Those with hyperkyphotic posture had about a 1.4 times higher rate of death, even after adjusting for other factors ([Kado et al., 2004, *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*, PMID 15450042](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15450042/)).
- In the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures, 1,196 women aged 65 and older were tracked for 15 years. Each additional 10 degrees of curve at the start was linked to a greater decline in how fast they could do five chair stands ([Katzman et al., 2013, *Journals of Gerontology Series A*, PMID 23633167](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23633167/)).
- Hyperkyphosis has also been linked with poorer lung function, lower physical function, and future fractures (Kado et al., 2007, above).

Posture is probably a marker of other things going on, not a single cause of bad outcomes. Still, a curve that keeps growing tends to travel with weaker legs and a harder time getting out of a chair, which is exactly what training is meant to protect.

## Can Strength Training Actually Improve Posture?

Yes, within limits. Here is what the best studies found.

| Study | Who | What they did | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| SHEAF randomized trial ([Katzman et al., 2017, *Osteoporosis International*, PMID 28689306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28689306/)) | 99 adults 60 to 88 (mean age 70.6) with a curve of 40 degrees or more | Group spine strengthening and posture training with a physical therapist, 1 hour, 3 times a week, for 6 months | Curve improved by 3 degrees more than in the education-only group; self-image improved; no group difference in physical function tests |
| Ball et al., 2009 (above) | Women 50 to 59 | Spinal extension exercises 3 times a week for 1 year, compared with women who did not do them | Curve progression was smaller in women who did the exercises; this part was not randomized |
| Sinaki et al., 2002 ([*Bone*, PMID 12052450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12052450/)) | 50 postmenopausal women 58 to 75, followed 8 years after a 2-year randomized back-strengthening trial | Progressive back-extension strengthening for 2 years | Back strength still higher at 10 years; vertebral compression fractures 1.6% of vertebrae examined vs 4.3% in controls |

A 3 degree change sounds small. The SHEAF authors noted it is roughly the size of the change caused by a new vertebral fracture, only in the other direction. A systematic review of 13 studies in adults 45 and older found that 8 improved at least one measure of posture, though the data were too thin and mixed to pool into a single number ([Bansal et al., 2014, *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, PMID 23850611](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23850611/)).

So "fixes" needs an honest footnote. Training will not erase a long-standing curve, and it will not undo a fracture. What it does well is slow the drift, take back a few degrees, and make the muscles that hold you upright stronger, which also matters for your spine and your balance.

## What Should You Do This Week?

If you have osteoporosis, a known vertebral fracture, or back pain that is new or getting worse, check with your doctor or a physical therapist before starting. Avoid loaded forward bending and twisting if you have low bone density. We cover that in more detail in [which exercises are safe with osteoporosis](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-11-exercises-safe-with-osteoporosis).

1. **Train your back extensors 2 to 3 times a week.** Start with prone back extensions on the floor: lie face down, lift your chest a few inches, hold 3 seconds, lower. Do 2 sets of 8 to 10. Bird dogs and supported hip hinges also train these muscles.
2. **Pull more than you push.** Rows, band pull-aparts, and face pulls work the muscles between and behind your shoulder blades. Aim for 2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15, twice a week, and do at least as many sets of pulling as pressing.
3. **Practice tall posture against a wall.** Stand with your heels, hips, upper back, and the back of your head touching a wall, chin slightly tucked. Hold 30 seconds, rest, repeat 3 times. This is the posture-training half of the SHEAF program, and it teaches you what upright feels like.
4. **Open the front of your chest.** A doorway pec stretch for 30 seconds on each side, once or twice a day, eases the pull of tight chest muscles. Our [stretches for back and neck tightness from desk work](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-08-19-stretches-for-back-and-neck-tightness-from-desk-work) add a few more options.
5. **Break up long sitting.** Every 30 to 45 minutes, stand, roll your shoulders back, and do 5 slow chin tucks. It is not a cure, but it interrupts hours in one rounded position.
6. **Keep the rest of your strength program going.** Squats, carries, and hinges train the legs and hips, which the 15-year data suggest are the first things to slip when posture worsens. Strength work also supports [bone density after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-03-05-how-strength-training-builds-bone-density-after-50), which protects the spine itself.

Give it time. The trials that showed changes ran 6 months to 2 years, so expect to judge progress over months, not weeks.

## Key Takeaways

- The forward curve of the upper back tends to grow with age, and one study found the biggest difference between women in their 50s and their 60s.
- Hyperkyphosis affects an estimated 20% to 40% of older adults, and many of them do not have vertebral fractures.
- Weak back extensor muscles are linked to a larger curve, and unlike bone loss or disc changes, that strength is something you can build.
- In a 6-month randomized trial of adults 60 and older, spine strengthening plus posture training reduced the curve by about 3 degrees compared with education alone.
- Women who built back strength for 2 years still had stronger backs and fewer vertebral fractures 8 years later.
- Training slows and modestly reverses posture changes; it does not erase a long-standing curve, so start early and stay consistent.
- If you have osteoporosis or a known spinal fracture, get cleared and avoid loaded forward bending and twisting.
