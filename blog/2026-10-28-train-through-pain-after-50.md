# Should You Train Through Pain After 50? Simple Rules for When to Push and When to Stop

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Most adults over 50 have heard one of two rules about pain in the gym: no pain, no gain, or stop the moment anything hurts. Neither one holds up. Some discomfort during training is normal and safe, and some pain is a clear signal to stop. After 50, when most people carry at least one cranky knee, shoulder, or back, knowing the difference is what keeps you training for years instead of weeks.

## Does Pain During Exercise Mean You're Doing Damage?

Not necessarily. Pain is a protective signal, not a damage meter. A stiff knee that aches on the first few squats and settles as you warm up is telling you something very different from a shoulder that gives a sharp pinch every time your arm goes overhead.

The research on long-term joint and muscle pain backs this up. A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 7 randomized trials with 385 people living with chronic musculoskeletal pain, comparing exercise programs that allowed or encouraged some pain with programs kept pain-free ([Smith et al., 2017, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID 28596288](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-097383)). The programs that allowed pain produced a small but significant benefit for pain in the short term. In the medium and long term, the authors found no significant difference between the two approaches for pain, function, or disability.

Read that carefully. It does not say that hurting more makes you better. It says that working into some discomfort did not make people worse, and that pain-free training also worked. For you, that means a little discomfort is not a reason to quit, and it is not something you need to chase either. These trials were in chronic pain, not fresh injuries, so the rules below apply to familiar aches, not a joint you hurt yesterday.

## What's the Difference Between Soreness and Pain?

Muscle soreness from training usually shows up a day or two after a session and fades over the next few days. It is the dull, everywhere ache that makes stairs a little slower. Pain that deserves your attention tends to look different. Use this as a quick sorting tool:

| Signal | Usually fine to keep training | Stop that exercise and reassess |
|---|---|---|
| Where you feel it | In the muscle you worked, often on both sides | In a joint or tendon, one specific spot, one side |
| What it feels like | Dull, achy, tight, tired | Sharp, stabbing, pinching, burning, or shooting |
| During the set | Stays the same or eases as you warm up | Gets worse with each rep or set |
| How you move | Normal movement | You start limping, shifting, or guarding |
| Next morning | Same or better than before the session | Worse, stiffer, or swollen |
| Other signs | None | Swelling, giving way, numbness, tingling, or bruising |

We cover the normal side of this in more depth in our post on [residual awareness vs soreness](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-03-19-should-you-be-sore-after-workout-after-50).

## Does Training Through Pain Make an Injury Worse?

Not if the load is managed. One of the clearest tests of this comes from tendon rehab. In a randomized trial, 38 people with Achilles tendinopathy all followed the same progressive calf and tendon loading program ([Silbernagel et al., 2007, *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID 17307888](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17307888/)). One group was allowed to keep running and jumping, guided by a pain-monitoring model that set limits on how much pain was acceptable. The other group stopped those activities for the first six weeks. Both groups improved significantly, there was no significant difference in the rate of improvement between them, and the authors found no negative effects from continuing activity.

That is one small trial in one condition, so it is not proof for every joint. It does fit with what we see in the studio: tendons and arthritic joints tend to respond better to steady, sensible loading than to long stretches of complete rest. Tendons also adapt more slowly than muscle, which is why [tendons age differently than muscles](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-05-27-why-tendons-age-differently-than-muscles) and why patience matters after 50. The same logic runs through [whether you should exercise with arthritis](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-17-should-you-exercise-with-arthritis).

## What Are Simple Rules for When to Push and When to Stop?

Rate your pain from 0 to 10, where 0 is none and 10 is the worst you can imagine. Then use these rules. They are our practical version of the pain-monitoring approach physical therapists use in rehab. The exact numbers are a guide, not a law.

- **0 to 2: train normally.** This is background noise for most adults over 50.
- **3 to 5: you can continue, with conditions.** The pain should stay steady or ease during the session, it should feel achy rather than sharp, and it should not change how you move.
- **Above 5, sharp, or climbing set to set: stop that exercise.** Not the whole session. Switch to a version that stays in the 0 to 5 range, or move on to a different movement.
- **The next-morning check.** By the morning after, the area should be back to where it was before you trained, or close. If it is worse, scale back the next session for that area.
- **The week-to-week check.** Over a week or two, your pain should hold steady or come down. If it keeps creeping up, the plan needs to change, even if each single session felt fine.

The next-morning and week-to-week checks matter more than how a single set feels. A joint can feel fine during the workout and tell you the truth the next day.

## How Do You Modify an Exercise Instead of Skipping It?

Stopping an exercise rarely means stopping training for that body part. You have several levers, and you usually only need to pull one:

- **Shorten the range.** If deep squats bother your knee, squat to a higher box and lower it over weeks. Our guide on [how to squat without knee pain after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-06-02-how-to-squat-without-knee-pain-after-50) walks through this.
- **Reduce the load.** Drop the weight 20 to 30 percent and rebuild over two to three weeks.
- **Slow the tempo.** A three-second lowering phase with a lighter weight is often better tolerated than fast reps with a heavier one.
- **Change the angle.** If pressing overhead pinches, try a landmine press or an incline press. If your shoulder has been stiff for months, read [what causes frozen shoulder after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-10-19-what-causes-frozen-shoulder-after-50) before you push range.
- **Swap the exercise.** A deadlift from the floor can become a kettlebell deadlift from a raised surface. A lunge can become a step-up.
- **Use a hold.** Holding a position you can tolerate, like a wall sit at a comfortable depth, keeps the muscle working while you find a range that does not hurt.

If several areas are flaring at once, or your whole body feels beaten up, the issue may be your total training load rather than one exercise. That is a good time for a [deload week](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-08-23-what-is-a-deload-week-and-do-you-need-one-after-50).

## When Should You Stop and See a Clinician?

These rules are for familiar aches and pains. Some signs mean you should stop and get checked rather than train around anything:

- Chest pain or pressure, unusual shortness of breath, or dizziness during exercise. Stop right away and seek urgent care.
- A pop, a sudden sharp pain, or a fall followed by swelling, bruising, or trouble bearing weight.
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness that spreads down an arm or a leg.
- A joint that is hot, red, and swollen.
- Pain that wakes you at night or is there at rest, unrelated to movement.
- Pain that has not improved after about two weeks of sensible modification.

None of this is a diagnosis, and we do not diagnose in the studio. If something on that list sounds familiar, talk with your doctor or a physical therapist. A good coach will then build your training around what they find.

## Key Takeaways

- Pain during exercise is a protective signal, not a reliable measure of damage, and some discomfort during training is normal after 50.
- A meta-analysis of 7 trials in chronic musculoskeletal pain found that exercise allowing some pain gave a small short-term benefit over pain-free exercise, with no difference in the medium or long term.
- In a randomized trial of people with Achilles tendinopathy, continuing activity with pain monitoring led to similar improvement as six weeks of rest from those activities, with no negative effects found.
- Pain that is dull, steady, and settles by the next morning is usually fine to train with, while sharp, worsening, or movement-changing pain means stop that exercise.
- A practical rule is to keep pain at 5 out of 10 or below, check how the area feels the next morning, and make sure pain is not climbing week to week.
- Modifying range, load, tempo, angle, or exercise choice usually lets you keep training a painful area instead of skipping it.
- Chest symptoms, spreading numbness or weakness, a hot swollen joint, or pain after a fall or pop are reasons to stop and see a clinician.
