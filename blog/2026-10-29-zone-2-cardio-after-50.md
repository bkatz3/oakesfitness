# What Is Zone 2 Cardio and Do You Need It After 50?

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

Zone 2 has been sold as the one cardio intensity that matters: a slow, steady effort that rebuilds your mitochondria and turns you into a fat-burning machine. That oversells it. Zone 2 is simply easy aerobic work, and it makes a very good base after 50, but it is not the only intensity that counts, and for most people training a few hours a week it should not be the only thing they do.

## What Is Zone 2, Exactly?

Zone 2 is an easy-to-moderate aerobic effort that sits below your first lactate threshold, the point where your body starts producing lactate faster than it clears it. In plain terms, you are breathing a bit harder than at rest, you could hold a conversation, and you could keep going for a long time.

There is no single official definition. Different coaches and labs use three-zone, five-zone, or seven-zone models, and "zone 2" in one system does not always match "zone 2" in another. A lab can pinpoint the threshold with a lactate or breath-gas test, but you do not need one to train well. What matters is the feel: steady, comfortable, conversational.

## Where Did the Zone 2 Craze Come From?

Mostly from elite endurance athletes. A widely cited review found that nationally and internationally competitive athletes training 10 to 13 times a week did about 80% of their sessions at low intensity (around 2 mmol of blood lactate) and about 20% dominated by high-intensity work ([Seiler, 2010, *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*, PMID 20861519](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/)).

That is a useful finding, but their easy volume is huge because their total volume is huge. Copying the 80% part without the volume leaves you with a lot of easy minutes and not much else.

## Is Zone 2 Better Than Harder Cardio?

Not as a rule. A 2025 review in *Sports Medicine* looked specifically at the claim that zone 2 is the best intensity for building mitochondria and fat-burning capacity in the general population ([Storoschuk et al., 2025, *Sports Medicine*, PMID 40560504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/)). The authors noted that the zone 2 recommendations lean heavily on observational data from high-volume elite athletes. They concluded the current evidence does not support zone 2 as the optimal intensity for those adaptations, and that intensities above zone 2 matter for cardiometabolic benefit, especially when total training time is limited.

That last point is the one that matters for most adults over 50. If you have three or four hours a week for everything, including strength work, spending every cardio minute at an easy pace leaves fitness on the table.

The best long-term data in older adults point a similar way. In the Generation 100 trial, 1,567 adults in Norway aged 70 to 77 were randomized to two supervised sessions a week of high-intensity intervals (around 90% of peak heart rate), two sessions a week of moderate continuous training (around 70% of peak heart rate), or a control group asked to follow national activity guidelines, and followed for five years ([Stensvold et al., 2020, *BMJ*, PMID 33028588](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33028588/)). Combined, the two training groups did not have lower mortality than the control group. Mortality trended lower in the interval group (hazard ratio 0.63) and slightly higher in the moderate group (hazard ratio 1.24), but neither difference was statistically clear. Many people in the control group also chose high-intensity activity on their own, which blurred the comparison.

The fair reading: in already active people in their 70s, two structured sessions a week did not clearly change survival, both intensities were workable, and the trend leaned toward including some harder work. It is not proof that steady cardio is useless or that intervals are required.

## Does Easy Cardio Still Work After 50?

Yes, especially if you are starting from little. A meta-analysis of 41 controlled trials with 2,102 sedentary adults aged 60 and older found that endurance training raised VO2max by an average of 3.78 mL/kg/min, about 16% more than controls ([Huang et al., 2005, *Preventive Cardiology*, PMID 16230876](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16230876/)). Gains were larger with programs longer than 20 weeks and at moderate intensities of about 60% to 70% of VO2max.

So steady, moderate work is a proven way to build aerobic fitness after 50. It is also easier to recover from, kinder to joints that do not love pounding, and something you can do the day after a hard lifting session. Those are good reasons to keep it, not reasons to make it the whole plan.

## How Do You Find Your Zone 2 Without a Lab?

Use the talk test first and heart rate second.

The talk test holds up well. In a study of 16 healthy, moderately active adults tested on both a treadmill and a bike, the point where reciting a standard passage first became difficult lined up almost exactly with the ventilatory threshold measured by gas analysis ([Persinger et al., 2004, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, PMID 15354048](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15354048/)). When speech was clearly uncomfortable, people were consistently above that threshold. Zone 2 sits right below that point: you can speak in full sentences, but you would not want to sing.

Heart rate is a useful check, with two caveats. First, the old 220 minus age formula underestimates max heart rate in older adults. A meta-analysis of 351 studies with 18,712 subjects, confirmed in a lab study of 514 healthy adults, found 208 minus 0.7 times age fits better ([Tanaka et al., 2001, *Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, PMID 11153730](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153730/)). For a 60-year-old, that is about 166 beats per minute, not 160. Second, any formula is an average, and individuals can sit well above or below it. Many coaches use roughly 60% to 70% of estimated max as a starting range for zone 2. Treat it as a rough guess and let the talk test overrule it.

Some medications, including beta-blockers, blunt your heart rate response, so heart-rate targets will read low. If you take one, go by the talk test and ask your doctor what effort range is right for you.

| Signal | Zone 2 (easy aerobic) | Harder work (above zone 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Talking | Full sentences, a little breathy | A few words at a time |
| Breathing | Deeper but controlled | Fast, hard to steady |
| Feel after the session | Could have kept going | Glad it is over |
| Rough heart rate guide (age 60) | About 100 to 116 bpm | Above your zone 2 range |
| How long you could hold it | 45 to 90 minutes | Minutes, in intervals |

## How Much Zone 2 Should You Do?

Here is a simple way to use zone 2 this week. Check with your doctor first if you have a heart condition, have been inactive for a long time, or get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath with effort.

1. **Start with two or three easy sessions of 20 to 30 minutes.** Brisk walking, an incline treadmill, a bike, a rower, or swimming all work. Pass the talk test the whole time.
2. **Build toward 150 minutes a week** by adding about 10 minutes a week. Most of these minutes can be zone 2.
3. **Once 150 easy minutes feels routine, make one session harder.** Swap it for intervals, such as 4 to 6 rounds of 2 minutes hard and 2 minutes easy. Our post on [whether HIIT is safe after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-09-24-is-hiit-safe-after-50) covers how to start.
4. **Keep roughly 80% of your cardio easy and 20% harder.** That borrows the useful part of the elite model without the elite volume.
5. **Put zone 2 on lifting days or the day after.** Easy cardio recovers well and rarely gets in the way of strength work. If you are doing both in one session, see [should you do cardio before or after lifting](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-03-08-cardio-before-or-after-lifting-after-50).
6. **Do not drop strength training to make room.** Cardio and lifting do different jobs after 50, which we covered in [can strength training replace cardio for heart health](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-10-23-can-strength-training-replace-cardio-for-heart-health-after-50).

## Key Takeaways

- Zone 2 is easy aerobic work below your first lactate threshold: steady, conversational, and sustainable for a long time.
- The zone 2 trend comes largely from elite endurance athletes, who do about 80% of their sessions at low intensity but train 10 or more times a week.
- A 2025 review in *Sports Medicine* found the evidence does not support zone 2 as the best intensity for building mitochondria or fat-burning capacity in the general population.
- In a meta-analysis of 2,102 sedentary adults aged 60 and older, endurance training raised VO2max by about 16%, with moderate intensities working well.
- In a five-year trial of 1,567 adults aged 70 to 77, both interval and moderate training were workable, and mortality trended lower with intervals, though not conclusively.
- The talk test closely tracks your ventilatory threshold; for heart rate, 208 minus 0.7 times age estimates max heart rate better than 220 minus age in older adults.
- After 50, make most of your cardio easy, add one harder session a week once you have a base, and keep lifting.
