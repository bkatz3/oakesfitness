# How to Return to Lifting After a Hip Replacement

*By The Oakes Fitness Team*

A lot of people assume a new hip means lifting is over, or that walking will bring the leg back on its own. Neither is true. The new joint usually fixes the pain, but the muscles around it can stay smaller and weaker for years unless you train them. After 50, when muscle is already harder to keep, that gap is worth closing on purpose.

## Does the Leg Get Strong Again on Its Own?

Not fully, at least not for many people.

A study of 20 patients with arthritis in one hip used CT scans to measure the muscles around both hips before surgery, at six months, and two years after the replacement ([Rasch et al., 2009, *Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery British Volume*, DOI 10.1302/0301-620X.91B5.21477](https://doi.org/10.1302/0301-620X.91B5.21477)). Two years out, compared with the healthy side, the operated side's iliopsoas, the main hip flexor, was 7.0 percent smaller and the hip adductors were 8.4 percent smaller. The gluteal muscles on the operated side also had lower density on the scans, which researchers read as poorer muscle quality. The authors called it persistent muscle atrophy and suggested more intensive rehab might reverse it.

That is a small study, but the pattern matches what many people notice. The hip stops hurting, and yet stairs, getting up from the floor, and standing on that leg still feel weaker than they should. Usually that is a muscle problem, and muscle responds to training.

## Is Heavy Strength Training Safe After a Hip Replacement?

In the right hands and at the right time, the research is encouraging.

A randomized trial put 60 hip replacement patients into either standard physiotherapy or maximal strength training ([Winther et al., 2018, *Acta Orthopaedica*, DOI 10.1080/17453674.2018.1441362](https://doi.org/10.1080/17453674.2018.1441362)). The strength group did leg press and hip abduction with the operated leg at 85 to 90 percent of their maximum, 4 sets of 5 reps, three times a week, at a municipal physiotherapy clinic for up to three months after surgery. At three months they were 43 kg stronger on the leg press and 3 kg stronger in hip abduction than the standard group. At six months the gaps were 30 kg and 3 kg. The authors reported that patients tolerated it well.

Two details matter. First, this was done at a physiotherapy clinic as part of formal rehab, not by people loading up on their own. Second, by one year the two groups were no longer different. Heavy early work sped up the gains, but it did not lock them in. What keeps the strength is training that continues after formal rehab ends.

## What About Hip Precautions?

This is where your surgeon's instructions come first, every time.

After some types of hip replacement, patients are told to avoid certain positions for a period, such as bending the hip past 90 degrees, crossing the legs, or turning the knee inward. A 2023 review pooled 4 randomized trials and 5 other studies, 8,835 patients in total, who had the posterior surgical approach ([Korfitsen et al., 2023, *Acta Orthopaedica*, DOI 10.2340/17453674.2023.11958](https://doi.org/10.2340/17453674.2023.11958)). It found routine precautions did not clearly change the rate of early dislocations, and the authors said the evidence does not support prescribing them to everyone. They also said higher quality studies could change that.

Read that as a reason to ask your surgeon which restrictions apply to you and for how long, not as permission to ignore them. Approach, implant, and your own anatomy all factor in. Once you know your limits, the training plan is built around them.

## How Long Until You Can Lift or Play Sports Again?

Longer than PT, shorter than most people fear.

A review of 37 studies looked at sport and work after hip replacement ([Hoorntje et al., 2018, *Sports Medicine*, PMID 29691754](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29691754/)). On average, 82 percent of people returned to the sport level they had before their hip symptoms started. The time to return to sport ranged from 16 to 28 weeks across studies. Those figures cover all kinds of activity and all kinds of patients, so treat them as a rough guide, not a deadline.

## What Do the Stages Look Like?

The timing for each stage is set by your surgeon and physical therapist. What follows is how we think about the order once they have cleared you.

| Stage | Goal | Example exercises |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rehab with your PT | Walking, range of motion, basic control | Whatever your PT prescribes |
| 2. Rebuilding the base | Get both legs working under bodyweight | Sit-to-stand from a high chair, hip bridge, side-lying leg raise, standing calf raise |
| 3. Adding load | Strength in the patterns you use every day | Leg press, box squat to a high box, step-up, cable or band hip abduction, kettlebell deadlift from a raised surface |
| 4. Long-term strength | Keep building, and prepare for the activities you want back | Split squat, single-leg work, heavier presses and hinges, carries |

The hip abductors, the muscles on the outside of the hip, deserve special attention. They keep your pelvis level when you stand on one leg, which is every step you take. If balance has slipped, our post on [how to improve balance after 50 to prevent falls](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-07-06-how-to-improve-balance-after-50-to-prevent-falls) has a simple progression to add from stage 2 on.

## How to Return to Lifting Once You're Cleared

Start only once your surgeon and physical therapist have cleared you for independent strength training. Ask them three things: which positions to avoid, for how long, and whether any lift is off the table for you. Within those limits, here is a plan you can use this week.

- **Train the legs 2 to 3 days a week**, with at least a day between sessions.
- **Pick 4 or 5 exercises** from the stage you are in, at least one for the front of the thigh and one for the outside of the hip.
- **Do 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps**, stopping each set with 2 or 3 good reps left.
- **Keep depth inside your limits.** A box squat or leg press lets you set a range that stays clear of deep hip bending. Raise the box rather than forcing depth.
- **Check both sides.** Single-leg moves like step-ups show you how far the operated leg lags. Add a set for that side if it is clearly behind.
- **Progress one thing at a time.** When every set feels solid, add a rep or two, or about 5 percent load.
- **Watch the next day.** Some soreness that settles is normal. More swelling or pain the next morning means the session was too much, so scale it back next time.

The general rules in [should you train through pain after 50](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-10-28-train-through-pain-after-50) apply here too: keep discomfort dull and steady, and stop anything sharp or anything that changes how you move. If you have had a knee replaced as well, or are weighing it, our post on [how to rebuild strength after a knee replacement](https://oakesfitness.com/blog/2026-11-03-rebuild-strength-after-knee-replacement) follows the same approach.

## When Should You Check Back With Your Surgeon or PT?

Training does not fix everything after a joint replacement. Stop and get checked if you notice:

- A hip or wound that is hot, red, draining, or newly swollen, or a fever.
- Calf pain, swelling, or warmth in the lower leg.
- A clunk, a feeling of the hip slipping, or a leg that suddenly looks shorter or turned.
- New groin or thigh pain, or pain that gets worse with each session instead of better.
- Pain that wakes you at night or has not settled after about two weeks of easier training.

We do not diagnose in the studio. If something on that list sounds familiar, talk to your surgeon or physical therapist first, and build the training around what they find. ## Key Takeaways

- A CT study of 20 patients found the hip flexor and adductor muscles on the operated side were still 7 to 8 percent smaller than the healthy side two years after hip replacement.
- In a randomized trial of 60 patients, clinic-based heavy leg press and hip abduction training made the operated leg markedly stronger at three and six months than standard physiotherapy.
- That early advantage was gone by one year, so continued training after rehab is what holds on to the strength.
- A 2023 review of 8,835 posterior-approach patients found routine hip precautions did not clearly change early dislocation rates, but your surgeon decides which restrictions apply to you.
- A review of 37 studies found about 8 in 10 people returned to their pre-symptom sport level, typically 16 to 28 weeks after surgery.
- Once cleared, 2 to 3 leg sessions a week of 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps, with extra attention on the outside of the hip, is a practical way to close the gap.
- New heat, swelling, calf pain, a feeling of the hip slipping, or worsening pain are reasons to check with your surgeon or physical therapist before training on.
